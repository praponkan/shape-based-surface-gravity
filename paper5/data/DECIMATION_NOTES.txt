DECIMATION NOTES
Shape-based surface gravity series, Paper IV supplementary note
Prapon Kanjanatarayont, August 2026

This note was written after Paper IV was published. It documents two
limitations of the mesh decimation used there, states what they do and do not
affect, and records the measurements that establish this. Nothing in Paper IV
is retracted or corrected by it.


1. WHAT PAPER IV USED

Meshes were reduced by vertex clustering, implemented as decimate_cluster in
surface_slope_lib.py. The method snaps every vertex to a grid, replaces each
occupied cell by the centroid of the vertices in it, rebuilds the faces from
the snapped indices, and discards faces that have collapsed onto two vertices
or that duplicate another face. The grid resolution is taken from a fixed
ladder and the first value that yields enough distinct cells is used.

That function is unchanged in this release. A script that ran against the
published version will produce the same numbers.


2. LIMITATION ONE: TOPOLOGY IS NOT PRESERVED

Two pieces of a surface that lie close together in space, such as the two
sides of a thin neck, can fall into the same grid cell and be merged. The
reduced mesh then has edges shared by more than two faces, and is no longer a
closed two-manifold.

Measured on a synthetic contact binary with a deep neck, 9,408 faces:

    reduction    faces    Euler chi    repeated directed edges
        25%       7,074        2                0
        43%       5,368       20               96
        66%       3,168       18               44
        77%       2,159       12               30

A closed, oriented two-manifold has every directed edge appearing exactly once
with its reverse present, and an Euler characteristic of 2 for a single
component. The onset is between a quarter and a half reduction, and the damage
does not diminish beyond it.

On the real bodies used in this series:

    body      native      reduced    repeated edges    chi
    Itokawa   49,152      41,369            21           8
    Itokawa   49,152      21,196            69          25
    Bennu    196,608      62,876            94          32
    67P    1,309,996      57,607         1,449         613

Undecimated meshes are unaffected: Itokawa at 49,152 and Eros at 49,152 both
have chi = 2 with no repeated or unmatched edges.


3. LIMITATION TWO: SMALL TARGETS ARE NOT REACHED

Because the grid resolution comes from a fixed ladder rather than being solved
for, the requested face count is a lower bound that is often not approached.
On the same synthetic body, requests for 1200, 800, 500 and 300 faces all
return the identical 2,159-face mesh. Any study quoting a target face count
should quote the count actually obtained; Paper IV does.


4. WHAT THIS DOES NOT AFFECT

A polyhedral volume is a sum of signed tetrahedra. A polyhedral potential and
its gradient, in the Werner-Scheeres formulation, are sums over oriented
facets. None of these consults connectivity: they need the facets to tile the
boundary with correct orientation, not to be stitched together. A mesh with
repeated edges therefore yields the same volume, potential, gravity and slope
as it would if the same facets were properly joined.

This was verified rather than assumed. Recomputing the reference Itokawa
inversion with a clipper that shares intersection points between adjacent
facets, rather than duplicating them, changed the recovered densities not at
all, the contrast by 8e-5 per cent and the excess mass by 1e-5 per cent.

Accuracy was checked independently against a homogeneous ellipsoid, whose
surface gravity has a closed form (MacMillan 1930), so that neither mesh nor
code enters the reference. Three ellipsoids, three spin states and five facet
counts, ninety configurations, gave the mean absolute error against the
analytic value:

                       mean slope    normalised dispersion
    vertex clustering      3.92%             0.07%
    quadric collapse       4.05%             0.09%

The two methods are equivalent in accuracy, with clustering marginally ahead.
The fields Paper IV reports are not in question.

The same test bears on Paper IV's central claim, that the normalised
geopotential dispersion is far less sensitive to mesh resolution than the mean
surface slope. Measured against the analytic ellipsoid rather than against a
finer mesh:

    faces     slope error    dispersion error    ratio
     2,000       11.62%           0.196%          59
     4,000        4.74%           0.117%          40
     8,000        2.30%           0.049%          47
    16,000        0.93%           0.028%          33
    32,000        0.33%           0.017%          19

The dispersion is 19 to 59 times the more accurate. Paper IV inferred a factor
of roughly 3 to 14 from mesh-to-mesh variation, so its conclusion holds and is
conservative.


5. WHAT THIS DOES AFFECT

Anything that needs to know which faces meet at a vertex:

  - vertex normals, which are averaged over the faces around a vertex;
  - inward offsetting, used to build a core-and-shell interior model;
  - surface flow or diffusion on the mesh;
  - any finite-element treatment;
  - a manifold test applied by a downstream tool, which will fail.

Work of that kind should not use a clustered mesh.


6. WHAT IS PROVIDED HERE

  decimate_qem.py
      Quadric edge collapse with the link condition enforced on every
      collapse, so the result is a closed two-manifold by construction. It
      also returns the requested face count exactly. Run
      "python decimate_qem.py --selftest" to verify it in place.

  surface_slope_lib.py
      decimate_cluster is unchanged. Two functions are added:
      decimate(V, F, target, method) dispatches to either method, defaulting
      to "cluster"; mesh_topology(F) reports the directed-edge audit and the
      Euler characteristic. The command line accepts --decimator qem.

Cost: quadric collapse takes about eight minutes to reduce a 1.3-million-facet
mesh to 50,000 faces, against under a second for clustering, and needs the
decimated mesh to be cached if it will be used more than once.


7. RECOMMENDATION

  - To reproduce Paper IV, change nothing. The default is the published
    behaviour.
  - For fields only, either method is suitable; clustering is much faster.
  - Where the mesh will be used for anything requiring connectivity, use
    --decimator qem, and check with mesh_topology that the result has
    chi = 2 and no repeated or unmatched edges.
  - Report which decimation was used and the face count obtained, not the one
    requested. The numerical value of a slope statistic depends on both.


8. AN OPEN QUESTION

On the four real bodies of the Paper IV resolution study, the mean slope under
clustering rises monotonically with facet count on every one of them, by
+21.9%, +14.2%, +5.8% and +1.8%. Under quadric collapse there is no monotonic
trend. On the smooth ellipsoid neither method shows such a drift: both
overestimate the slope on coarse meshes by the same amount and converge
smoothly.

The natural explanation is surface roughness, which an ellipsoid does not
have. That is a hypothesis. No analytic reference exists for a rough body, so
which decimation is closer to the truth on a real asteroid cannot presently be
established, and no claim is made here that either is.
